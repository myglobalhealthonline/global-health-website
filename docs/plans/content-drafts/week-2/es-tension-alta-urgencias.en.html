High blood pressure symptoms and emergencies

A high number matters, but signs of acute organ injury are what turn a reading into a medical emergency.

High blood pressure usually has no symptoms. You cannot confirm or rule it out by how you feel. The urgent question is different. If a very high reading occurs with chest pain, severe breathlessness, one-sided weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, confusion, collapse, sudden loss of vision or a sudden severe headache, call 112 in Spain. These can be signs of acute injury to the heart, brain, lungs, eyes or major blood vessels. Do not wait for tea or garlic to work, and do not take an extra prescription dose unless your own clinician has given you a specific written plan for that exact situation.

First principle

What symptoms can high blood pressure cause?

The honest answer is that it usually causes none. Measuring it is the only way to know the number.

Headache, dizziness, ringing in the ears, nosebleed, palpitations or blurred vision can occur when pressure rises, but none is specific. The same symptoms occur with anxiety, migraine, fever, pain, dehydration, sleep loss and many other problems. Feeling perfectly well is not reassuring either: blood pressure can remain raised for years without announcing itself.

Separate two questions. ‘Do I have hypertension?’ needs repeated measurements and clinical assessment. ‘Could something dangerous be happening now?’ requires a search for acute warning signs and an assessment of the whole situation.

  • A familiar headache does not prove that blood pressure is its cause.
  • Dizziness does not prove the pressure is high; it can also occur when pressure is low.
  • Pain or panic may temporarily raise a reading, but an extreme result still deserves attention.
  • Sudden neurological, breathing or chest symptoms change the priority immediately.

The Spanish Heart Foundation describes hypertension as a largely silent condition detected by measurement.

The critical distinction

A very high reading does not always mean the same emergency

A hypertensive emergency is not defined by the number alone. It means severe elevation accompanied by acute organ injury or symptoms that suggest it.

You cannot confirm organ injury at home. Clinicians may need an examination, ECG, blood and urine tests, an eye examination or imaging to assess the heart, brain, kidneys, retina and aorta. What you can recognise is a group of warning signs that should be treated as dangerous until assessed.

A repeated reading around 180/120 mmHg or above is markedly high. With no warning symptoms it is not automatically an organ-damage emergency, but it still needs prompt same-day medical advice. With warning symptoms, stop repeatedly checking the monitor and call 112.

Do not try to label ‘urgency’ versus ‘emergency’ yourselfNo pain does not prove that organs are safe, and a lower number does not rule out stroke, an acute coronary syndrome or an aortic emergency. Sudden symptoms and change from normal matter as much as the reading.

The 2024 ESC guideline distinguishes markedly elevated blood pressure from a hypertensive emergency with acute organ damage.

Do not wait

When to call 112 for high blood pressure in Spain

Call 112 when a high reading occurs with sudden symptoms involving the chest, breathing, brain, vision or circulation.

  • Chest pain, pressure or burning, especially with cold sweat, nausea or pain into an arm, back, neck or jaw.
  • Severe breathlessness, laboured breathing, choking at rest or coughing pink froth.
  • A drooping face, one-sided weakness or numbness, or new trouble speaking, understanding, walking or coordinating movement.
  • New confusion, a seizure, fainting or reduced consciousness.
  • Sudden loss of vision, new double vision or an explosive headache unlike your usual headaches.
  • Sudden tearing pain in the chest, back or abdomen, or rapid deterioration during pregnancy or after delivery.

Do not drive yourself if you have neurological symptoms, chest pain, breathlessness, visual disturbance or fainting. Note the time symptoms began, unlock the door if you are alone and keep your medication list nearby. Follow the emergency operator’s instructions.

Spain’s General Council of Pharmacists includes hypertensive crises in its community-pharmacy urgent referral guidance.

If you are stable

What to do with a very high reading and no warning symptoms

If you feel stable and have none of the warning signs, sit quietly for a few minutes and repeat the measurement once using proper technique.

  • Support your back and feet, keep legs uncrossed and rest the arm at heart level.
  • Make sure the cuff fits and sits on bare skin rather than clothing.
  • Do not talk during the measurement; record both numbers, the time and any symptoms.
  • If the repeat remains around 180/120 mmHg or higher, obtain urgent same-day medical assessment even if you feel well.
  • If a warning sign begins while you wait, change course and call 112.

For non-urgent follow-up, organise readings with the blood pressure record. It is not a dose calculator and must never delay emergency care. Its purpose is to give a clinician a clearer pattern later.

If the repeat is lower, keep the first result. Arrange review if the event was new, keeps recurring or came with symptoms. A video appointment can review stable readings and history, but cannot examine an emergency or replace tests that require an in-person setting.

Avoid extra harm

Herbal tea, garlic, lemon and tablets: what not to do

No folk remedy reliably treats a possible hypertensive emergency, and forcing the number down rapidly can be unsafe.

People search for herbal teas, garlic, lemon, cold water or breathing techniques because they want an immediate action. Sitting and breathing calmly while arranging help is reasonable, but it does not treat a stroke, heart attack, pulmonary oedema or aortic injury. Large amounts of water, salt, alcohol or herbal products can also conflict with other conditions and medicines.

Do not take someone else’s captopril, nifedipine or other antihypertensive. Do not double a dose, take the next dose early or revive an old ‘rescue’ instruction unless your own clinician has set out an individual plan for this exact situation. A sudden fall can reduce blood flow to organs already at risk.

After the episode

When to start with a GP and when cardiology helps

General practice coordinates most stable cases; cardiology helps when control remains difficult, symptoms persist or tests suggest cardiovascular disease.

A general practice and chronic-care appointment can review measurement technique, home records, current prescriptions, adherence, side effects and related risks such as diabetes, kidney disease, cholesterol or weight. That is the right route when no emergency signs are present.

An online cardiology appointment in Spain can help interpret an existing ECG, Holter report, echocardiogram or hospital letter and assess difficult-to-control hypertension. It cannot replace emergency care or hands-on investigations.

You can also view the medical team in Spain or contact Global Health Spain if you are unsure which appointment fits. If warning signs are present, use 112 instead.

FAQ

Questions about high blood pressure and emergencies

What symptoms does high blood pressure cause?

Often none. Headache, dizziness, palpitations and blurred vision can occur but are not specific. Measurement identifies the number; the combination of reading, sudden onset and signs of acute organ injury determines urgency.

Should I call 112 for a reading of 180/120?

Call 112 if it comes with chest pain, breathlessness, neurological symptoms, confusion, fainting, sudden visual loss or a sudden severe headache. Without these symptoms, repeat once correctly and seek urgent same-day assessment if it persists.

Can I take an extra blood pressure tablet?

Not unless your own clinician has given you a specific plan for that exact situation. Doubling, advancing or combining medicines can cause a dangerous fall and does not by itself treat possible organ injury.

Will herbal tea or garlic lower it quickly?

They are not reliable treatments for an acute rise and never replace 112 or clinical assessment. Home remedies may delay care, and some herbal products interact with medicines or existing disease.

Can an online appointment deal with a high reading?

It can review a stable home record and arrange follow-up, but it cannot examine acute organ injury. Warning signs require 112 or in-person emergency care; without them, general practice is usually the first step.
